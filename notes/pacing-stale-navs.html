Pacing a private program when the NAVs are a quarter old

Reported NAVs lag public markets. Nowcast them before you size next year's commitments.

Cristian Arenas28 October 2026Test it in the Private Capital Lab

Private fund NAVs arrive about a quarter late. When public markets fall, the public side of the portfolio reprices the same day and the private marks don't. The private allocation looks high, and a pacing model run on those numbers says commit less.

That answer is usually too cautious. The allocation is high because the denominator fell, and the private marks will follow it down over the next two quarters. A committee that trims a vintage on stale marks gives up a vintage year it cannot get back.

The method

The nowcast comes first, because every later number divides by total assets.

  1. I take each fund's last reported NAV and count how many quarters stale it is.
  2. I regress the fund's reported quarterly returns on a public index at lags of zero, one and two quarters. The sum of the slopes is the fund's economic beta. The lags matter because appraisal marks catch up slowly. A fund with too short a history gets a prior for its strategy.
  3. I roll the NAV forward through each stale quarter. I add the calls, take off the distributions and apply beta times the index return. A stale mark gets no credit for manager skill, so alpha is zero. The band around the nowcast widens with each stale quarter.
  4. I compute the allocation on nowcast NAV and hand that total to the pacing rule.
  5. The pacing rule picks the commitment that puts projected private NAV on target at the end of the horizon, after the existing funds call and distribute. Each fund follows the Takahashi-Alexander model.
  6. I hold that plan fixed and run it through a Monte Carlo with public returns, private returns, calls and distributions drawn together. The bad paths size the cash buffer.
Nowcast, each stale quarter k:
  NAV_k = (NAV_k-1 + calls_k - dists_k)
          x (1 + B x r_k)
  r = public index return
  B = b0 + b1 + b2, fitted from
  r_fund,t = a + b0 r_t
           + b1 r_t-1 + b2 r_t-2

Allocation:
  private % = NAV / (public + NAV)

Pacing, commit c in year t, horizon h:
  NAV / total assets = target
  at the end of year t + h - 1,
  with c in each year t to t + h - 1

Worked example: the lab's base case

The nowcast

The lab holds a made-up $1bn endowment with 20 funds, $482m committed and $101m unfunded. The target is 25% with a band of 20% to 30%. GP marks put private NAV at $279.7m, which is 28.0% of total assets. Eighteen funds are marked at 30 June 2026 and two at 31 March. The lab's public proxy returns for the last two quarters are -8.0% and -5.0%, and the median fund beta is 0.79.

Rolled forward, program NAV falls 6.3% to $262.2m, with a 90% band of $244m to $281m. Public assets are $720m, so total assets come to about $982m and the allocation is 26.7%. That is still a little over target, but part of the excess has gone. Even the top of the band, $281m, is no higher than the reported $280m.

Telmira Growth II has a beta of 0.67 and falls 8.7% to $17.6m. Five funds, Ostrel Capital Partners VII the largest, rest on a strategy prior and carry the softest estimates.

The pace

On nowcast total assets the pacing rule sets the 2027 commitment at $63.4m, then $58.1m in 2028 and $54.1m in 2029, $176m over three vintages. The program distributes more than it calls in 2027, $73.5m against $34.9m with the first call on the new vintage included, so the near-term budget runs above the steady-state need of 3.98% of total assets a year. The plan path stays between 23.8% and 25.7% for ten years.

On the reported marks the program looks 3.0 points over target, and the rule gives less. The program then drifts under target once the marks catch up.

The buffer

The Monte Carlo runs the fixed plan over 600 paths and 40 quarters. The median allocation in three years is 24.2%, with a 10th to 90th range of 18% to 31%. In 65% of paths the allocation crosses 30% in some quarter, almost always right after a public drawdown. In 11% of paths liquid public assets fall below unfunded commitments. That second number sets the cash buffer.

The liquidity stress cuts public assets 25%, calls 35% of unfunded in 12 months and cuts distributions 60%. Calls come to $35.4m (35% of $101.1m), distributions to $19m and spending to $44m (4.5% of $982m). The net cash need is $61m, and liquid public assets cover it 6.2 times. I hold at least $130m in cash and short Treasuries, about twice that need.

After a public sell-off, a private allocation above target is mostly a denominator effect. I nowcast the marks before I let that number cut a vintage, and I size the cash buffer from the shortfall paths.

What changes the answer

Expected public return

The pacing rule targets a share of total assets, so the budget depends on how fast the public side grows. Lower the expected public return in the lab and the 2027 budget falls. Raise it and the budget rises. I set it to the policy return the board already uses.

Public volatility

The plan does not change with volatility, because the rule is deterministic. The fan does. Raise public volatility and the chance of a liquidity shortfall climbs quickly, while the odds of a band breach rise more slowly. I stress this input before I agree to a cash floor.

Pacing horizon

A shorter horizon asks the rule to close the gap sooner, so the 2027 budget rises a little. A longer one eases it. The default is four years because new commitments reach peak NAV about then.

Pitfalls

  • Pacing on reported marks right after a sell-off. The budget gets cut on a share that is about to fall.
  • Treating the nowcast as a point estimate. Its band is widest when the marks are most stale.
  • Feeding reported volatility into the Monte Carlo. For Quorvane Buyout III the first-order autocorrelation is 0.44, and desmoothing lifts volatility from 8.9% to 14.2%. Smoothed inputs make the fan too narrow.
  • Expecting an index beta to catch a write-down at one company. I read each statement when it arrives and rerun the plan.
  • Pacing on positions that do not tie. Brisolt Credit Opportunities III has an open roll-forward break of $0.70m: its statement lines support $22.35m of NAV against a stated $23.05m. The program carries the lower figure, and I close the gap before I size a Brisolt re-up.
  • Selling into a breach the marks will fix. In the stress the allocation jumps to 31.8% on lagged marks and is still 30.4% once marked down. Secondary discounts widen in a sell-off, so I assume 20%. Selling 10% of the book at that discount raises $20m and costs $5m against NAV. I would pay that only if the breach outlasted the next two sets of marks.

What I check

  1. The NAV date of every fund, with anything two or more quarters stale flagged.
  2. The calls and distributions since each NAV date, from notices and the cash ledger. For a fund still investing, like Caldrin Infrastructure II, they can outweigh the market.
  3. Each fund's beta, its R squared and how many quarters of history it rests on. A strategy prior means a soft estimate.
  4. The program nowcast and its band against reported NAV. If the top of the band is still below reported NAV, the direction is settled.
  5. The allocation, the room below the upper band and the pacing budget, all on nowcast NAV.
  6. The shortfall probability at base and at higher public volatility, and the cash floor against the stressed 12-month need.
  7. Every statement roll-forward. A fund with an open break stays out of the ledger until the GP answers.
  8. A rerun date for when the next quarter's marks arrive.
