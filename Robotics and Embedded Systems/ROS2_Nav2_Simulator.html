Guide — ROS 2 Nav2 Simulator

1 · What this simulator is

A from-scratch reimplementation of the ROS 2 Nav2 navigation pipeline running entirely in the browser: no ROS, no rosbag, no hardware. The differential-drive robot is modelled on a TurtleBot3 Waffle (footprint radius 0.22 m, max_vel_x 0.26 m/s, max_vel_theta 1.82 rad/s, acc_lim_x 2.5 m/s², acc_lim_theta 3.2 rad/s²). The world is a 2-D axis-aligned map of walls and obstacles; the simulation runs at a real-time factor of 3.0× so a 0.26 m/s robot is watchable, which is why the on-screen clock advances faster than wall time.

2 · Costmap & inflation layer

Both costmaps use a 0.05 m grid, the Nav2 default. Cell cost comes from the standard inflation-layer formula applied to the Euclidean distance d from the cell to the nearest obstacle surface:

ParameterValueMeaning
resolution0.05 mglobal & local costmap cell size
inscribed_radius0.22 mrobot footprint radius; d ≤ r ⇒ cost 253
inflation_radius0.55 mcost decays to 0 beyond this
cost_scaling_factor3.0exponential decay rate

cost = 253 · exp(−3.0 · (d − 0.22)) for 0.22 m < d < 0.55 m; cells inside an obstacle are lethal (254). Walls (static layer) and obstacles (obstacle layer) are rasterised into one 8-bit grid, and d comes from a 3×3 chamfer distance transform over that grid — the same O(cells) trick nav2_costmap_2d uses instead of a brute-force distance query, which is what makes every planner and controller lookup O(1). The whole grid is rebuilt at 5 Hz, so moving obstacles genuinely change the cost the planner sees. The Global Costmap button draws the entire grid; the Local Costmap button draws the 3 m × 3 m rolling window the controller actually checks, with its live count of cells at or above the inscribed cost.

3 · Global planner (planner_server)

A* over the inflated costmap, 8-connected, expanded on a 0.20 m search lattice (four costmap cells) so the search stays interactive. The heuristic is the octile distance in metres with unit step cost — exactly admissible and consistent for 8-connected motion, and used unweighted (f = g + h), so the returned path is optimal on that lattice. Any cell whose cost is ≥ 253 (inscribed or lethal) is rejected outright, so the path can never cross a lethal cell; the remaining inflation cost is added to g as a penalty, which pushes the path towards the centre of free space instead of hugging walls. The result is then shortened by a line-of-sight pass that re-checks the same costmap.

4 · Local controller (controller_server)

Regulated pure pursuit. Steering curvature is κ = 2·sin(α) / L_d where α is the heading error to the lookahead point and L_d is the lookahead distance, and the command is ω = κ·v. The lookahead is velocity-scaled, L_d = clamp(v · 1.5 s, 0.30 m, 0.90 m). Three regulation heuristics reduce v: curvature (below a 0.90 m turning radius), proximity (inside the inflation layer) and approach (linear ramp inside 0.6 m of the goal). Above a heading error of 0.785 rad the controller rotates in place first. Both v and ω are then rate-limited by the acceleration limits above. Nav2 also ships DWB (trajectory rollout with critics) and MPPI (sampling-based model-predictive control) as alternative controller plugins; only regulated pure pursuit is implemented here.

5 · AMCL-style localisation

A 500-particle filter runs beside the simulator. Particles are propagated with the sampled odometry motion model (δrot1, δtrans, δrot2 with noise coefficients α1..4), weighted by an 8-beam likelihood-field endpoint model against analytically raycast ranges, and low-variance resampled whenever N_eff = 1/Σw² falls below N/2. Like AMCL it only updates after update_min_d = 0.20 m or update_min_a = 0.20 rad of motion. Press P and click to teleport the robot: the cloud is re-scattered with 0.5 m / 0.5 rad spread and you can watch it reconverge. It is called AMCL-style and not AMCL because the particle count is fixed (no KLD adaptive resampling) and, honestly, the controller still drives on ground truth — the filter is observed, not closed into the loop.

6 · Behaviour tree (bt_navigator)

The tree executed is the shape of Nav2's navigate_to_pose_w_replanning_and_recovery.xml, and the sidebar highlights the node that is actually ticking:

RecoveryNode (NavigateToPose)
├─ PipelineSequence
│   ├─ RateController 1 Hz → ComputePathToPose
│   └─ FollowPath
└─ RoundRobin (recoveries)
    ├─ ClearEntireCostmap
    ├─ Spin 1.57 rad
    ├─ BackUp 0.15 m
    └─ Wait 5 s

The RateController re-plans once a second and immediately if a waypoint inside the local window turns lethal. If planning fails, the round robin runs the next recovery in the list and re-ticks the pipeline; it only gives up after all four have been tried without a path. That is why placing an obstacle on the path first produces a silent replan, and walling the robot in produces the full recovery sequence. One honest caveat: ClearEntireCostmap rebuilds the grid rather than wiping an accumulated obstacle layer, because obstacles here are ground truth rather than remembered sensor marks — it returns SUCCESS but rarely changes anything, so in practice Spin and BackUp do the useful work.

Methodology & assumptions

What this models

The ROS 2 Nav2 navigation pipeline, reimplemented in plain JavaScript. A static map of axis-aligned walls plus dynamic obstacles is rasterised into a 0.05 m costmap and inflated with a chamfer distance transform, rebuilt at 5 Hz. planner_server runs A* over that costmap with an admissible octile heuristic; controller_server runs a regulated pure pursuit controller inside TurtleBot3 Waffle velocity and acceleration limits; bt_navigator ticks the navigate_to_pose_w_replanning_and_recovery tree, replanning at 1 Hz and falling through a round robin of recoveries when no path exists. A 500-particle AMCL-style filter estimates the pose alongside, and a rolling 3 m local costmap is rebuilt at 5 Hz from live obstacle positions.

Key assumptions

  • Robot: TurtleBot3 Waffle, differential drive, footprint radius 0.22 m, max_vel_x 0.26 m/s, max_vel_theta 1.82 rad/s, acc_lim_x 2.5 m/s², acc_lim_theta 3.2 rad/s².
  • Costmaps: resolution 0.05 m, inflation_radius 0.55 m, cost_scaling_factor 3.0, lethal 254 / inscribed 253; static + obstacle layers rasterised into one grid and inflated by a 3×3 chamfer distance transform, rebuilt at 5 Hz; local window 3 × 3 m.
  • Planner: A* on a 0.20 m 8-connected lattice over that costmap, octile heuristic, unweighted f = g + h, 12 000 expansion cap, replanning at 1 Hz (RateController).
  • Controller: lookahead_time 1.5 s clamped to [0.30, 0.90] m, regulated_linear_scaling_min_radius 0.90 m, rotate_to_heading_min_angle 0.785 rad, rotate_to_heading_angular_vel 1.8 rad/s, control loop 20 Hz.
  • Localisation: 500 particles, motion-model coefficients α1..4 = 0.05 / 0.05 / 0.05 / 0.02, 8-beam likelihood field (zhit 0.85, σhit 0.12 m, zrand 0.15), update_min_d 0.20 m, update_min_a 0.20 rad, resample when Neff < N/2.
  • LiDAR: 120 beams over 360° drawn from the 360-sample LDS-01 pattern, 3.5 m max range, analytic ray/AABB and ray/circle intersection, no noise on the rendered scan (the filter adds σ = 0.02 m).
  • Determinism: mulberry32 PRNG, seed 20260909, reseeded on every reset. Simulation advances at a real-time factor of 3.0×.

Formulas & references

  • Inflation layer: cost = 253 · exp(−cost_scaling_factor · (d − inscribed_radius)) for inscribed_radius < d < inflation_radius, with d from a two-sweep 3×3 chamfer distance transform (1, √2 weights) — within a few percent of the exact Euclidean distance.
  • Octile heuristic: h = (dx + dy) + (√2 − 2)·min(dx, dy) — admissible and consistent for 8-connected grids with unit/√2 step costs.
  • Pure pursuit: κ = 2·sin(α) / L_d, ω = κ·v, with curvature, proximity and approach regulation (Macenski, Moore, Lu, Merzlyakov & Ferguson, 2023, From the desks of ROS maintainers: a survey of modern & capable mobile robotics algorithms in the robot operating system 2).
  • Particle filter: sampled odometry motion model and likelihood-field range model, low-variance resampling, N_eff = 1 / Σw² (Thrun, Burgard & Fox, 2005, Probabilistic Robotics, ch. 5, 6 and 8).
  • Behaviour tree and lifecycle architecture: Macenski, Martín, White & Clavero (2020), The Marathon 2: A Navigation System (IROS).

Limitations

  • The controller drives on ground truth, not on the AMCL-style estimate. The particle cloud is displayed and scored honestly, but localisation error is never fed back into planning, so the demo cannot show a robot getting lost.
  • No real ROS: no DDS, no lifecycle transitions, no action servers or preemption, no TF buffering or extrapolation. The transform tree and topic rates in the sidebar are labels, not live publishers.
  • A* runs on a 0.20 m lattice over the 0.05 m costmap, so the plan is optimal on that lattice, not on the full-resolution grid. Nav2's NavFn/Smac planners search the full costmap.
  • Kinematics only: no mass, wheel slip, motor dynamics or IMU; collisions simply stop the robot rather than being resolved physically.
  • 2-D, axis-aligned world with perfectly reflective surfaces; the laser has no dropout, mixed pixels or incidence-angle effects, and the costmap has no sensor field-of-view or clearing raycasts — every obstacle is always visible, whereas a real obstacle layer only marks what the LiDAR currently sees.
  • Distances behind the inflation layer come from a chamfer transform on cell centres, so cost boundaries can be off by up to half a cell (2.5 cm).
  • ClearEntireCostmap rebuilds the grid instead of clearing remembered sensor marks (there are none), so that recovery almost always succeeds without changing anything.
  • Simulation runs 3× faster than wall time so a 0.26 m/s robot stays watchable; on-screen distances and velocities are physical, elapsed times are not.
Browser simulation for education. Timings, register values and sensor models are idealised approximations and are not hardware-validated.
