Guide — Portable Alpha Simulator

1 · The idea

A traditional 60/40 fund buys stocks and bonds with all of its capital. A portable alpha programme gets the same 60/40 exposure with index futures (or total-return swaps), which only require a few percent of initial margin. The freed-up capital is invested in an alpha sleeve (a hedge-fund style, low-beta return stream) and the remaining cash / T-bills earns the cash rate while serving as collateral. With a 100% notional the identity is simply PA = Benchmark + A·(alpha net of fees) − financing frictions: the alpha is "ported" on top of the benchmark.

2 · Beta sleeve

Futures prices already embed financing at the cash rate, so the sleeve earns Nt · (Benchmark − Rf). On top of that the model charges the financing spread (implied repo above SOFR, 30–60 bp for equity index futures in recent years, close to zero for Treasury futures, so the input is a blended number), the roll execution cost and a zero-mean basis noise. Both the spread and the basis are procyclical: roll richness and index-versus-future dislocation widen precisely when you would want to step away, so the model scales the spread 2.5× and the basis 3× in a crisis. Setting the notional above 100% is levered beta on the same terms. Nt carries a subscript because the notional is not a constant: it is whatever the collateral account can still margin (section 4).

3 · Alpha sleeve

The sleeve behaves like a diversified hedge-fund book: gross monthly return = Rf + alpha + βα · (Equity − Rf), where alpha is drawn with mean IR × vol / 12 and standard deviation vol / √12. A management fee accrues monthly and a performance fee is taken on gains above the sleeve's high-water mark. Expected alpha decays linearly with the alpha decay rate to mimic crowding. Regime behaviour is fixed and stylised on 2008 and 2022:

With the default 6% vol sleeve a crisis run of six months or more produces a sleeve trough of roughly −18% (median across 800 seeds), in line with the 2008 experience of hedge-fund composites. The last three columns are the financing side of a stress: margin requirements, roll richness and basis all move against a levered holder at the same time as the alpha stream fails. That simultaneity is the whole point.

4 · Collateral, margin and the way these programmes actually fail

The collateral account is a real balance carried month to month, not a ratio recomputed from scratch. It starts at (1 − A) of NAV in T-bills, earns Rf, and every month it pays or receives the futures variation margin. It is not topped up from the alpha sleeve until that sleeve's redemption window opens, which is the input most people get wrong: a quarterly fund with 45 days' notice cannot fund tomorrow's margin call.

Three things then have to line up in a bad month:

  • Variation margin is daily, not monthly. Settling once a month understates how much cash a drawdown actually consumes, because it ignores the path inside the month. The model samples the intra-month low of the futures P&L exactly, from the Brownian bridge that ends at the realised month return: min = (R − √(R² − 2σ² ln U)) / 2 with U uniform. A month that ends down 2% may well have been down 6% on the way.
  • Initial margin rises in stress. Clearing houses raise performance bonds when volatility spikes — CME lifted E-mini requirements roughly 60% between February and March 2020. The requirement here is scaled 1.6× in a crisis and 1.25× in a rate shock, so the collateral you need goes up while the collateral you have goes down.
  • The escape routes are finite. A shortfall is met first from cash, then from a committed credit line sized to the liquidity buffer (one month at Rf + spread + 100 bp). If both are exhausted the programme has no choice: it cuts futures notional down to what the remaining collateral can margin, paying 5 bp of traded notional in calm markets and 20 bp in a crisis. That cut happens at the intra-month low, and the notional stays cut until the next redemption window — so the programme sells beta at the bottom and buys it back higher.

This is forced de-leveraging, and it is what closed most 2008-vintage portable-alpha programmes: the alpha sleeve was gated exactly when the beta overlay was calling for cash. The health panel counts these months, the average notional actually carried, and the minimum collateral cushion on the path. At the default 50% alpha allocation none of it triggers; raise the allocation towards 85% or the notional towards 130% and it does. That is the trade-off the simulator exists to show.

5 · Reading the results

  • Growth / drawdown charts show the benchmark and the programme on the same seeded path; shaded bands are stress regimes.
  • Tracking error = annualised standard deviation of (PA − benchmark) monthly returns; information ratio = annualised active return / tracking error; Sharpe = (annualised arithmetic return − Rf) / annualised volatility; max drawdown = worst peak-to-trough fall of the $100 path.
  • Return decomposition sums the arithmetic annualised monthly contributions; the net differs slightly from the geometric CAGR in the table. The alpha sleeve gross bar includes the Rf earned inside the sleeve, the collateral bar the Rf on the rest. The beta bar is the notional actually carried times (benchmark − Rf), so it falls short of N × (benchmark − Rf) whenever the programme was de-levered.
  • One path is not an answer. On a 3% tracking error the standard error of a 15-year active return is close to 0.9%/y, so the chart you are looking at is one draw out of a very wide distribution. The health panel therefore re-runs the same programme on 96 alternative market paths and reports the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of the active return, and how often it is positive. Read those two rows before reading anything else in the KPI table. On the shipped defaults the median path adds only a few tenths of a percent a year and the 10th-to-90th band is several percent wide — and the default seed happens to sit near the bottom of that band. It has been left there rather than swapped for a flattering one.

Methodology & assumptions

What this models

A 180-month (15-year) Monte-Carlo comparison of a stock/bond benchmark with a portable-alpha programme on the same seeded path. Market months come from a three-state Markov chain (calm, 2008-type crisis, 2022-type rate shock) with regime-specific means, volatilities and equity–bond correlation, and with procyclical margin, financing and basis. The programme holds the benchmark synthetically through index futures, keeps A of NAV in a fee-paying hedge-fund style alpha sleeve with a stated equity beta, and runs a stateful collateral account: variation margin is paid out of real cash, the intra-month low of the futures P&L is sampled from a Brownian bridge because margin settles daily, the account is only topped up from the sleeve on its redemption dates, and when cash plus a committed credit line can no longer margin the position the programme is forced to cut notional at the low. Growth, drawdown, KPIs, a contribution waterfall, calibration and liquidity checks, a cross-seed distribution of the active return, and one-month stress scenarios are shown.

Key assumptions

  • Calm regime: equity +1.10%/mo, σ 3.7%/mo; bonds +0.45%/mo, σ 1.3%/mo; correlation +0.10. Unconditionally, at the 15% stress setting, the chain delivers a 13.7% realised stress share, equities ≈ 8.1% arithmetic / 14.8% vol, bonds ≈ 4.2%, the 60/40 benchmark ≈ 6.6% arithmetic / 9.3% vol (measured over 300 seeds), cash 4.0% constant.
  • Crisis (mean 12 months): equity −3.0%/mo, σ 7.0%; bonds +0.6%/mo, σ 1.8%; correlation −0.35; alpha drift −0.4σ/mo, alpha vol ×2, alpha beta ×1.5; initial margin ×1.6, financing spread ×2.5, basis ×3.0.
  • Rate shock (mean 9 months): equity −1.0%/mo, σ 5.0%; bonds −1.2%/mo, σ 1.8%; correlation +0.60; alpha drift ×0.25, vol ×1.3, beta ×1.5; initial margin ×1.25, financing spread ×1.5, basis ×1.8.
  • Futures execution cost on notional traded (forced de-leveraging and rebalances): 5 bp in calm, 10 bp in a rate shock, 20 bp in a crisis.
  • Defaults: 60/40, N = 100%, financing spread 35 bp blended over both legs, roll 5 bp/y, basis 10 bp/mo, A = 50%, alpha vol 6%, gross IR 1.0, alpha beta 0.15, fees 1.5% / 20% above high-water mark with no hurdle, alpha decay 1.0%/y, drag 15 bp/y, initial margin 6% of notional (a 60/40 blend of ~6–8% equity and ~1–3% Treasury futures margin), haircut 2%, liquidity buffer / credit line 5% of NAV, sleeve redemption cycle 3 months.
  • The programme rebalances to target weights only on the sleeve's redemption dates; between them the collateral account absorbs everything. Shocks are Gaussian; the cash rate is constant.

Formulas & references

  • NAV is carried as two balances, cash and sleeve, and the monthly return is NAVt/NAVt−1 − 1. Cash receives cash·Rf + Nt·(Bench − Rf) − N̄·(spread + roll) ± N̄·basis − liquidity costs; the sleeve compounds at Rf + α + βα(Eq − Rf) − mgmt − perf − drag. N̄ is the average of the notional before and after any forced cut.
  • Performance fee = rate × max(0, sleeve unit NAV − high-water mark), accrued and crystallised monthly on the unit NAV; the mark resets to the post-fee unit NAV, so subscriptions and redemptions do not reset it.
  • Intra-month low of the futures P&L, sampled exactly from the Brownian bridge ending at the realised excess return R with monthly benchmark variance σ²: min = (R − √(R² − 2σ² ln U))/2, U ~ U(0,1). Verified against the closed form P(min < a) = exp(−2a(a−R)/σ²).
  • Margin call when cash + Nt·min < marginregime·Nt/(1−haircut). The shortfall draws on the credit line at Rf + spread + 100 bp; beyond the line the notional is cut to (available collateral)·(1−haircut)/marginregime and stays cut until the next redemption date.
  • Sharpe = (12·mean − Rf) / (σ√12); tracking error = σ(PA − Bench)·√12; information ratio = 12·mean(PA − Bench) / TE; beta = cov / var; max drawdown on the cumulative path.
  • Kung & Pohlman (2004), Portable Alpha: Philosophy, Process, and Performance; Grinold & Kahn (2000), Active Portfolio Management; Fung & Hsieh (2004), Hedge Fund Benchmarks: A Risk-Based Approach.

Limitations

  • Synthetic Gaussian regime mixture, not historical data; fat tails only arise from regime switching. Jumps, gaps and limit-down sessions are not modelled, so the true tail of the collateral drain is worse than what is shown.
  • Constant cash rate; no term structure, no total-return-swap alternative, no taxes and no dividend-withholding effects on futures.
  • One blended margin rate for both legs, with no cross-margining offset between equity and Treasury futures — a real clearing member would net some of it, so the margin here is conservative.
  • Variation margin is checked once per month at the sampled intra-month low rather than day by day, and the credit line is assumed committed and always available at the stated rate. Both understate the number of calls; a real facility can be pulled precisely when it is needed.
  • Forced de-leveraging is modelled, but the alpha sleeve is assumed to honour redemptions in full on its redemption dates. Gates, side-pockets and suspensions are not modelled, and in 2008 they were the binding constraint: a gated sleeve turns a de-leveraged programme into a permanently de-leveraged one.
  • The alpha sleeve is one composite stream: no manager dispersion, no lock-ups, no notice periods beyond the redemption cycle, and performance fees crystallise monthly rather than annually, which overstates the fee drag on a choppy path.
  • Alpha is drawn independently of the equity shock beyond the stated beta and the regime shift, so tail dependence between the alpha stream and the market is only as strong as those two levers make it.
  • Regime parameters, stress multipliers and execution costs are hand-set stylisations of 2008, 2020 and 2022, not estimated from data.
  • A single 15-year path carries a standard error on the active return of roughly 0.9%/y at a 3% tracking error. Treat the cross-seed rows in the health panel as the result and the charted path as an illustration.
