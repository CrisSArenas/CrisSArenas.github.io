What you are getting into

You wake up on the intake deck of an industrial plant that has gone wrong. Three levels — Intake Bay, Coolant Works and The Furnace — each with keycard-locked doors, hidden rooms, coolant sludge that will take your health if you stand in it, and something at the bottom that is considerably larger than you are. Four weapons. Five difficulty levels, the mildest of which is genuinely mild and the harshest of which is not.

Between levels you get the tally: kills, items and secrets as percentages, and your time against a par time. It is scored the way you remember being scored.

How it works

The renderer

For each of the 320 screen columns the engine casts a ray and walks the level grid with a DDA until it hits a wall, then draws one vertical textured strip whose height comes from the perpendicular distance — using the perpendicular distance rather than the true distance is what keeps the walls flat instead of fish-eyed. Floors and ceilings are cast per pixel row. It all lands in a 320×168 software framebuffer, which is then blitted to the canvas with nearest-neighbour scaling so the pixels stay honest.

Light and depth

Brightness falls off with distance through a 32-level shade table, which is most of why it feels like the era rather than like a modern toy. Sprites — enemies, pickups, fireballs, blood — are billboards sorted back to front and clipped against a per-column depth buffer, so a husk standing behind a pillar is correctly hidden by it.

Doors that behave

Doors are not solid blocks that vanish. Each one is a thin plane at the middle of its cell, so while a door is sliding open a ray can pass through the gap and you can see the room beyond before you can walk into it. The ray-marcher steps to the door plane, checks whether the hit falls inside the remaining panel, and carries on if it does not.

Art from code

There is not a single image file. Fifteen wall, floor and ceiling textures are painted into offscreen canvases at load. Every enemy frame — three viewing angles, walk, attack, pain and a four-stage death — comes out of one parametric humanoid function; the four enemy types are just different palettes, proportions and weapons passed into it.

Sound from code

No audio files either. Every shot, grunt, door and pickup is synthesised live with Web Audio oscillators and filtered noise bursts shaped by envelopes. The low drone under the whole thing is two detuned oscillators through a lowpass filter with a very slow LFO on the pitch.

Levels that are provably finishable

Each level is authored as a grid of characters and run through a validator before it ships: every row the same width, a solid border, exactly one exit, and a flood fill that acquires keycards progressively to prove the blue key is reachable before the blue door, and so on down to red. An early draft of level three had a sealed room; the validator caught it.

Worth knowing

  • Push into a wall that looks slightly wrong and press use. Secret doors are textured to match the wall they sit in.
  • Armour absorbs a third of incoming damage until it runs out. Pick it up.
  • Green sludge hurts about five health a second. It is never the only route.
  • Firing is loud. Anything within about thirteen metres wakes up and comes looking.
  • There are cheat codes — type them while playing. They are not the ones you know, but they are the same idea, and the first is a synonym for invulnerable skin.
FURNACE is an original game. It is not DOOM. It contains no id Software code, art, audio, level data or trademarks, and it is not affiliated with or endorsed by id Software or ZeniMax. It is a homage, written from scratch, to the techniques of the early-1990s first-person shooter — grid raycasting, billboard sprites, distance-diminished lighting — with every piece of content generated procedurally in your browser.
