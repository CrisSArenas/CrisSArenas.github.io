A working price–time-priority matching engine with a synthetic order-flow model, and five execution algorithms working a parent order through it. Child orders join the queue at the touch and fill only when the size ahead of them is consumed, or cross the spread and pay for it. The shortfall is then decomposed off the tape, and the Almgren–Chriss frontier is drawn with η and γ regressed out of this market rather than assumed. Getting γ right is the interesting part: the obvious regression of price change on order flow is endogenous — flow leans toward fair value, so both move together — and it overstates the coefficient roughly fivefold. What identifies it is our own order, which is worked to a schedule and therefore exogenous. Drag the timeline to inspect the book at any second.

Guide — Limit Order Book & Execution Engine

1 · The matching engine

A real one, not a price series with a spread bolted on. Prices are integer ticks and each side of the book is a dense array indexed by tick, with the best bid and best ask held as pointers that walk outward when a level empties — which is how venues actually store a liquid instrument, because it makes the hot path O(1).

Matching is price–time priority. A marketable order walks the opposite side from the touch, consuming whole levels until it is filled or its limit stops it. Within a level the queue is first in, first out, and that detail is the entire reason passive execution is hard: when you post at the bid you are not at the front, you are behind everything already resting there.

2 · Queue position, and why it decides everything

The engine tracks, for each of your resting orders, the quantity ahead of it in its level. An incoming sell that takes 5,000 shares at your price consumes that 5,000 from the front of the queue: it eats the size ahead of you first, and only reaches you once that is gone. Cancellations are removed proportionally from ahead and behind, which is the standard approximation and roughly what happens in practice.

The consequence you can watch in the ladder panel: a passive order at a deep queue position can sit for minutes without a fill while the price walks away from it. That is adverse selection — you get filled quickly precisely when you did not want to be, and slowly when you did.

3 · The order-flow model

Every simulated second the engine draws:

  • Limit orders on both sides, Poisson in count, placed at a distance from an unobserved fair value that is exponentially distributed. Quoting around fair value rather than around the touch is what makes the book stable and gives it a realistic shape instead of collapsing or exploding.
  • Market orders, Poisson in count with a Hawkes-style self-excitation term so trades cluster, which real trades do. Direction is biased toward fair value, so order flow is partly informed and prices mean-revert to it — without that, impact would be pure noise.
  • Cancellations, as a hazard rate on resting size.

Fair value itself is a random walk scaled to the chosen regime's volatility. The emergent spread, depth and volume are not set directly; they come out of the arrival, cancel and trade rates.

4 · Identifying impact, and the thing that went wrong first

The first version of this page measured impact by running every configuration twice against an identical order-flow stream — once with the parent order and once without — and differencing the two mid-price paths. It is a clean idea and it does not work here. Common random numbers synchronise the inputs, not the state: the moment one fill differs the two books diverge, and the gap between the paths became a random walk with a standard deviation above 100 bps around an effect worth under 10. The measurement was noise.

What replaced it is what you could actually run against real data, and it has a textbook identification problem in the middle of it.

5 · Calibrating η and γ

Temporary impact, η. For each fill, the slippage against the mid prevailing when it printed. Because the comparison is contemporaneous, the price drift cancels and the estimator is quiet. Those volume-weighted slippages are regressed on execution rate across a sweep of constant-rate executions, giving cost = ε + ηv. The fitted ε lands within a cent of the measured half-spread, which is a useful check that nothing is broken. The fit is deliberately run out to very high participation, where you can see the points bend away from the line — real temporary impact is closer to a square root of rate than a straight line, and the chart shows that rather than hiding it.

Permanent impact, γ, and why the obvious estimator fails. The natural thing is to regress the change in mid on signed order flow — a Kyle lambda. On this market that returns a coefficient about five times too large, and the page prints it next to the good one so you can see the gap. The reason is endogeneity: market orders lean toward fair value, so flow and price are both driven by the same unobserved thing, and the regression credits flow with a move it did not cause.

The fix is an exogenous instrument, and there is one sitting right there: our own parent order. It is worked to a schedule and knows nothing, so its size is uncorrelated with fair value. Sweeping order size across seven levels for each of fourteen independent seeds, then differencing out a seed fixed effect to remove the background move common to that seed, leaves the variation in our own size to identify γ. The price change is read after a 150-second cool-down so the transient push has decayed and only the part that stays is measured — without that the coefficient still came in more than twice too high.

The panel prints the fitted γ with its t-statistic and R². On a model whose true coefficient is known, it recovers it to within about fifteen per cent.

Each one is also evaluated analytically through the Almgren–Chriss cost and variance functionals, so all five appear on the frontier chart as points and you can see how far each sits from the efficient boundary.

7 · Reading the shortfall

Implementation shortfall is the average execution price against the arrival mid, signed so positive is always worse for you. Every print decomposes exactly into what it cost against the mid at the time, and where that mid already was relative to arrival — so spread paid, temporary impact and price move while working sum to the shortfall with no residual, and the panel prints the residual to show it.

What is not a fourth bar is your own permanent footprint. It is not separable by measurement: it is already inside the price move while you were working, and no amount of arithmetic on your own fills can tell you how much of that move you caused. The page applies the calibrated γ to the flow you had already put through the book at each print and reports that as an attribution under the chart, labelled as an estimate. Anything that presents that split as a measurement is overselling it.

Methodology & assumptions

What this models

A single-name limit order book with price–time priority matching, driven by a synthetic order-flow model (Poisson limit arrivals placed around an unobserved fair value, self-exciting market orders biased toward that fair value, cancellation as a hazard on resting size, and a Kyle-style channel through which signed flow updates fair value). A parent order is worked through it by one of five execution schedules, posting passively at the touch and crossing to catch up. Temporary impact is estimated from contemporaneous fill slippage against rate; permanent impact is identified from exogenous variation in our own order size with a seed fixed effect and a cool-down window, because the naive contemporaneous regression is endogenous and overstates it about fivefold. The Almgren–Chriss cost–risk frontier is then drawn from those coefficients.

Key assumptions

  • One instrument, one venue. No fragmentation, no routing, no dark pools, no auctions, no latency and no queue jumping by faster participants.
  • Tick size $0.01 on a $100 stock. Levels are dense arrays over a ±$10 band; the best bid and ask are pointers that walk when a level empties.
  • Within a level the queue is FIFO. Cancellations remove size proportionally from ahead of and behind your order, which approximates a randomly chosen resting order being pulled.
  • Time is bucketed to one second, with several arrivals, trades and cancels interleaved inside each bucket.
  • Almgren–Chriss uses the discrete formulation of the 2000 paper: linear temporary and permanent impact, arithmetic Brownian price, and the exact closed-form cost and variance of the optimal trajectory.
  • Everything is deterministic in the seed. The same controls reproduce the same book, the same fills and the same decomposition.
  • Calibration runs 14 seeds × 7 order sizes over a 600-second window with a 150-second cool-down, which is about a hundred paired observations and roughly a second of compute at page load.

Formulas & references

  • Cost and risk: E = ½γX² + εΣ|nk| + (η̃/τ)Σnk², V = σ²Στxk², with η̃ = η − ½γτ.
  • Optimal trajectory: xj = X·sinh(κ(T−tj))/sinh(κT), with cosh(κτ) = 1 + ½κ̃²τ² and κ̃² = λσ²/η̃.
  • Almgren & Chriss (2000), “Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions”, Journal of Risk 3(2) — the trajectory and the closed-form cost and variance used here.
  • Cont, Stoikov & Talreja (2010), “A Stochastic Model for Order Book Dynamics”, Operations Research 58(3) — the arrival, cancel and market-order structure this flow model follows.
  • Perold (1988), “The Implementation Shortfall: Paper versus Reality”, Journal of Portfolio Management 14(3) — the benchmark the shortfall is measured against.

Limitations — read these before you read the numbers

  • The flow is synthetic. It is a zero-intelligence model with an informed drift, not a replay of real messages. It produces a plausible spread, depth profile and volume, and it responds to pressure in the right direction and roughly the right magnitude — but the absolute bps are a property of this model, not of any real stock.
  • Nobody reacts to you. Real counterparties detect a large child-order footprint and fade or front-run it. Here the flow is indifferent, so predictable schedules like TWAP are treated far more kindly than they would be.
  • No latency, no fragmentation, no fees. Orders arrive instantly, there is one venue, and there are no exchange fees or rebates — and maker rebates are frequently the difference between a passive strategy paying and not paying.
  • γ is identified, but not precisely. The within-seed estimator recovers the model's true permanent coefficient to within about fifteen per cent, with a t-statistic around two on roughly a hundred observations. That is enough to reject the naive estimate, which is five times too large, but it is not a tight number, and the own-impact attribution under the waterfall inherits that uncertainty.
  • η is fitted linearly and the data are not. The calibration sweep runs out past fifty per cent participation, where temporary impact bends below the straight line — real impact is closer to a square root of rate. The R² on that fit tells you how much that costs, and the points are plotted so you can see the curvature rather than take the coefficient on trust.
  • One path is one path. A single run's realised shortfall carries enormous sampling error relative to the expected cost. The frontier is an expectation; the red cross is one draw from a wide distribution.
