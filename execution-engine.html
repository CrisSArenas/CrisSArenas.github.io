How do you buy 200,000 shares without pushing the price up on yourself?

Every large order moves the market. Trade fast and you pay for the push; trade slowly and the price has time to drift away from you. This page runs a working limit order book, the matching engine at the centre of every stock exchange, and lets you work an institutional order through it using the five strategies professional trading desks use. Then it shows you what the trade cost, where every basis point went, and how much of it was skill and how much was luck.

How to read an order book

Bids
Shares people are waiting to buy. The best (highest) bid sits just below the middle.
Asks
Shares people are waiting to sell. The best (lowest) ask sits just above the middle.
Spread
The gap between the best bid and the best ask. Buy right now and you pay the ask, half the spread above the mid price.
Depth
How many shares wait at each price. Buy more than the best ask shows and you climb to worse prices.
Queue
At any one price, orders fill first come, first served. Join the bid and you wait behind everyone already there.

Estimates against the truth

Why the obvious regression gets permanent impact wrong

The natural way to measure permanent impact is to regress price changes on signed order flow, buying volume minus selling volume, and read off the slope (a Kyle lambda). On this market that slope comes out several times too large.

The reason is endogeneity. Some traders know where fair value is and buy when the price is below it. Their buying and the price rise that follows are both caused by the same thing, the gap to fair value, so the regression credits the order flow with a move it did not cause.

The fix is an input that cannot know anything about fair value. There is one: our own order. It follows a schedule, so its size is exogenous. Run the same session at seven sizes, difference out each session’s own move (a seed fixed effect), wait five minutes for the temporary push to fade, and the relationship between our size and the lasting price change identifies γ. That recovers the true value to within a few per cent.

How the simulator makes prices

  • A real matching engine. Prices are whole cents; each side of the book is an array indexed by price with pointers to the best bid and ask. Orders match by price, then time, and the engine tracks how many shares are ahead of yours in the queue.
  • Order flow arrives every second: limit orders placed around fair value, market orders that cluster in bursts (a Hawkes process), and cancellations as a hazard on resting size. Activity follows the intraday volume curve, fading through the morning.
  • Fair value is a random walk with the chosen volatility, drawn from its own random stream. That is what lets the simulator replay the identical session without your order, the counterfactual a real desk never has.
  • Your impact has two parts with known sizes: a permanent shift per share (γ) and a temporary footprint that decays with a one-minute half-life, the Obizhaeva–Wang picture of a book that refills, but not instantly.
  • Each session starts after three minutes of warm-up, so the book is at its natural depth when your order arrives.

Guide: Limit Order Book & Execution Engine

1 · What the page does

It simulates a single stock trading on one exchange, second by second, and lets you work a large order through it. The walkthrough goes: look at the market, set up the order and strategy, watch it trade, read the bill, compare all five strategies on identical sessions, and finally see how the impact model is estimated and how close the estimates get to the truth.

Nothing is pre-computed. Every session is generated in your browser from a seed, so the same settings always reproduce the same result.

2 · The matching engine

Prices are whole cents. Each side of the book is an array indexed by price, with the best bid and best ask held as pointers that move when a level empties, how exchanges store a liquid instrument, because it keeps every operation constant-time.

Matching is price–time priority. A buy that crosses takes the lowest asks first and walks up the book; within a price, the order that arrived first fills first. The engine tracks how many shares are ahead of your resting order, so it only fills once those are used up. Cancellations are removed proportionally from ahead of and behind you.

3 · The order flow

  • Limit orders arrive at a Poisson rate, placed a random number of ticks from where liquidity is centred.
  • Market orders cluster in bursts through a Hawkes-style self-excitation. Some are informed and trade toward fair value; the rest toss a coin.
  • Cancellations are a hazard on resting size, so depth settles at an equilibrium instead of draining.
  • All three follow the intraday volume curve by clock time, fading through the morning from 10:00.
  • Every session starts after three minutes of warm-up, so the book is at its natural depth when the order arrives.

4 · How prices form

Fair value is a random walk at the regime’s volatility, drawn from its own random stream: one draw a second, whatever else happens. That is what lets the simulator replay a session with your order removed and see the same news.

Permanent impact. Every uninformed share traded, and every share of yours, moves fair value by γ and it stays moved. Informed flow only closes the gap between the price and fair value, so it moves nothing.

Temporary impact. Each share of yours also shifts where liquidity is centred, and that footprint decays with a one-minute half-life, a book that refills, but not instantly (Obizhaeva & Wang, 2013).

Both coefficients are known, which is what lets step 6 score the estimators against the true values.

Each strategy rests a child order at the best price, sized to what its schedule still needs and never more than a share of the visible queue, and crosses the spread with a marketable limit order, never more than six cents through, when it falls behind.

6 · Reading the cost

Implementation shortfall splits exactly into six parts, with no residual: spread paid and book walked on crossings, spread earned on passive fills, your price impact, market drift, and unfilled shares at the closing price.

Separating your impact from market drift needs to know what the price would have done without you. A real desk never has that. The simulator does: it re-runs each session with the order removed, and the same fair-value path. That turns “what did my strategy cost” into something measured to a fraction of a basis point, while the market’s drift, which swings by tens of basis points, is reported separately as luck.

7 · Estimating impact the way you would on real data

No counterfactual, only prints. Constant-rate orders at seven sizes on each of fourteen sessions, all crossing. Within a session every size sees the same fair-value path, so a seed fixed effect removes the market’s move and our own size (exogenous, because it is scheduled) identifies the coefficients. γ comes from the price change after a five-minute cool-down; η from cost per share against rate, with the permanent half taken back out.

The naive Kyle regression of price change on all signed flow is shown next to it. It overstates γ several times over, because informed flow and the price move it anticipates are driven by the same gap to fair value.

Methodology & assumptions

What this models

A single-name limit order book with price–time priority matching and queue-position tracking, driven by a synthetic order-flow model: Poisson limit arrivals centred on fair value, self-exciting market orders split between informed and noise, cancellation as a hazard on resting size, and an intraday activity curve. Fair value follows its own random walk; uninformed and own flow move it permanently, and own flow leaves a transient footprint that decays. A parent order is worked by one of five schedules, resting at the touch and crossing to catch up. Costs are decomposed exactly, with own impact separated from market drift by replaying each session without the order.

Key assumptions

  • One instrument, one venue, a one-cent tick on a $100 stock. No routing, dark pools, auctions, fees or latency.
  • Regimes are set to measured annual volatility of 13%, 22%, 25% and 50%, with spreads of roughly one to three cents and between about 0.8 and 2.3 million shares traded in a morning half-hour, a liquid large-cap.
  • Time is bucketed to one second, with several arrivals, trades and cancels interleaved inside each second.
  • Transient impact decays with a one-minute half-life; permanent impact is linear in shares.
  • Every result is the mean of eight independent sessions. All five strategies see the same eight sessions.
  • Impact calibration runs 14 sessions × 7 sizes over ten minutes plus a five-minute cool-down, about a quarter of a second of compute per regime.

Formulas & references

  • Cost and risk: E = ½γX² + εX + (η̃/τ)Σnk², V = σ²Στxk², with η̃ = η − ½γτ.
  • Optimal trajectory: xj = X·sinh(κ(T−tj))/sinh(κT), cosh(κτ) = 1 + ½κ̃²τ², κ̃² = λσ²/η̃.
  • Almgren & Chriss (2000), “Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions”, Journal of Risk 3(2).
  • Obizhaeva & Wang (2013), “Optimal Trading Strategy and Supply/Demand Dynamics”, Journal of Financial Markets 16(1), the decaying transient footprint.
  • Kyle (1985), “Continuous Auctions and Insider Trading”, Econometrica 53(6), the lambda the naive regression estimates.
  • Cont, Stoikov & Talreja (2010), “A Stochastic Model for Order Book Dynamics”, Operations Research 58(3), the arrival, cancel and market-order structure.
  • Perold (1988), “The Implementation Shortfall: Paper versus Reality”, Journal of Portfolio Management 14(3).

Limitations: read these before you read the numbers

  • The flow is synthetic. It reproduces a plausible spread, depth, volume and volatility and responds to pressure in the right direction, but absolute basis points are a property of this model, not of any real stock.
  • Nobody reacts to you. Real counterparties detect a predictable child-order pattern and trade ahead of it. Here the flow is indifferent, so TWAP in particular is treated more kindly than it would be.
  • Impact is linear in rate. Real temporary impact is closer to a square root of participation. The transient footprint here also ramps up over a minute or two, which is why a constant-cost model fitted to it reads η about 15% low.
  • The counterfactual is a luxury. Splitting own impact from market drift is only possible because the simulator can replay a session without the order. Real TCA has to estimate that split from a model.
  • Eight sessions is a small sample. Trading costs are measured tightly; totals carry several basis points of noise.
