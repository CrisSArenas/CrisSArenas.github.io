Guide — Factor Replication & Multiple-Testing Lab

1 · The problem this lab is about

By 2012 Harvey, Liu and Zhu counted 316 published factors claiming to explain the cross-section of equity returns, and the rate of publication was accelerating. Their point was not that the individual papers were fraudulent. It was arithmetic: if hundreds of researchers test thousands of candidate signals against the same few decades of US equity data, and each one uses a 5% significance test, then a large number of “discoveries” are guaranteed to be noise even if every single researcher behaves impeccably.

The conventional hurdle is |t| > 1.96. That is the right hurdle for one pre-registered test. It is the wrong hurdle for the 1,001st test on the same data, and the factor literature is much closer to the second case than the first.

2 · What the numbers are, and what they are not

Each anomaly carries an annualised premium, volatility and original sample period calibrated to the magnitudes reported in the original papers. From those parameters the lab simulates a monthly return series with a seeded generator, with an AR(1) term so the errors are not independent, and then computes every statistic on the page — mean, Newey–West standard error, t, p, Sharpe, decay — from that series.

So the t-statistics are real computations on synthetic data, not numbers typed into a table. Change the window and they recompute properly. What they are not is a replication of any specific paper: this is not CRSP, it is a laboratory. The mechanism is exact; the individual figures are indicative.

3 · Newey–West standard errors

Factor returns are autocorrelated, so the ordinary standard error σ/√T is too small and the t-statistic too large. The lab uses the Newey–West heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent estimator with Bartlett weights:

S = γ0 + 2 · Σj=1..L (1 − j/(L+1)) · γj,  se = √(S/T)

with the standard automatic bandwidth L = ⌊4(T/100)2/9⌋. The table prints the plain and the HAC t side by side so the size of the correction is visible; on these series it typically shaves 4–9% off |t|.

4 · The four hurdles

RuleCritical |t|What it controls
None1.96The chance that this one test is a false positive. Says nothing about the other M−1.
BonferroniΦ−1(1 − α/2M)Family-wise error: the chance of any false positive among all M. Very strict, and valid whatever the dependence between tests.
Benjamini–Hochberglargest k with p(k) ≤ kq/MFalse discovery rate: the expected share of the rejections that are false. Less strict than Bonferroni and usually the right trade-off for factor research.
Harvey–Liu–Zhu3.0The authors’ rule of thumb after working through Bonferroni, Holm and BH on the actual publication record. A flat, memorable bar.

Bonferroni and BH both move with M, the number of candidate signals searched — which is the parameter nobody can observe, because failed tests are not published. That is the honest difficulty at the centre of this literature, and it is why the slider goes to 3,000 rather than sitting at 20.

5 · Post-publication decay

McLean and Pontiff (2016) took 97 published anomalies and compared returns inside the original sample, after the sample ended but before publication, and after publication. They found roughly a 26% fall in the first gap and about 58% after publication. Some of that is statistical — the original estimate was biased upward by selection — and some is economic: once a mispricing is documented, capital arrives and trades it away.

The lab gives each anomaly its own decay factor drawn around that average, so the “Post-publication” window is a genuinely different and much harsher test. Switching to it is the single most informative thing on the page: it is where a well-known name with a published t of 5 lands at 1.4 and stops being a factor.

6 · The noise-mining panel

The panel at the bottom generates signals with zero true premium and runs them through the same machinery. With no correction, about q × N of them will pass — five per hundred at the 5% level — and each one will look like a discovery, with a respectable t-statistic and a plausible-looking equity curve. Bonferroni usually admits none.

The counter above it, E[max |t|], is the expected largest t you would see from M pure-noise draws, approximated by √(2 ln 2M) − (ln ln 2M + ln 4π) / (2√(2 ln 2M)). At M = 1,000 it is about 3.2. If the best factor in your search has a t of 3.0 and you searched a thousand, you have found less than the null predicts.

7 · What this does not do

  • It does not model the correlation between tests. Real candidate signals overlap heavily — book-to-market and earnings-to-price are close cousins — which makes Bonferroni conservative and the effective number of independent tests much lower than M. The lab treats tests as independent, which is the standard simplification and an acknowledged one.
  • It does not do transaction costs, capacity or shorting constraints. Several anomalies on the list survive statistically and die economically, and the alpha-signal engine on this site covers that side.
  • It does not bootstrap. A block bootstrap of the maximum statistic, as in White’s reality check or Hansen’s SPA test, is the more rigorous route than a closed-form haircut.

Methodology & assumptions

What this models

A replication bench for the equity factor zoo. Twenty published anomalies are simulated at monthly frequency from parameters calibrated to their original papers, then re-tested with Newey–West standard errors under four decision rules: an uncorrected 5% test, Bonferroni family-wise control, Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery control, and the Harvey–Liu–Zhu t > 3.0 rule of thumb. The sample window can be set to the original publication sample, the post-publication period, or the full history, which exposes the McLean–Pontiff decay. A parallel experiment mines pure-noise signals under identical rules to quantify what each hurdle actually costs and buys.

Key assumptions

  • Returns are generated as rt = μ + ρ(rt−1 − μ) + σ√(1−ρ2)·εt with ε standard normal from a seeded mulberry32 generator, ρ between 0.03 and 0.16 per anomaly. The mean steps down from μ to μ(1−decay) in the month of publication.
  • Newey–West bandwidth L = ⌊4(T/100)2/9⌋, Bartlett kernel, applied to the demeaned series.
  • Two-sided p-values from the normal distribution, not Student’s t. With 200–700 monthly observations the difference is immaterial, and the multiple-testing literature works in the normal limit.
  • Tests are treated as independent. Real candidate signals are heavily correlated, so Bonferroni here is conservative and the effective number of independent tests is well below M.
  • Decay factors are drawn per anomaly around the McLean–Pontiff post-publication average of 58%, with dispersion; they are not the measured decay of any individual factor.
  • Everything is deterministic in the seed, so the page reproduces exactly on reload and any two readers see the same numbers.

Formulas & references

  • HAC variance: S = γ0 + 2Σj=1..L(1 − j/(L+1))γj; t = μ̂ / √(S/T). Newey & West (1987).
  • Bonferroni: reject when p < α/M, i.e. |t| > Φ−1(1 − α/2M).
  • Benjamini–Hochberg: sort p(1) ≤ … ≤ p(M), take the largest k with p(k) ≤ kq/M, reject the first k. Benjamini & Hochberg (1995).
  • Expected maximum under the null: √(2 ln 2M) − (ln ln 2M + ln 4π)/(2√(2 ln 2M)).
  • Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016), “…and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns”, RFS 29(1) — the t > 3.0 recommendation and the 316-factor count.
  • McLean & Pontiff (2016), “Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?”, Journal of Finance 71(1) — the 26% / 58% decay estimates.

Limitations — read these before you read the numbers

  • This is synthetic data. The anomalies are named after real published factors and parameterised to resemble them, but no CRSP, Compustat or vendor data is used anywhere on the page. A row that fails here has not been refuted; it has been simulated failing.
  • M is unobservable. Nobody knows how many signals were tried. The slider makes that uncertainty explicit rather than hiding a choice inside a footnote, but moving it changes the answer, and that is the honest state of the field.
  • Independence is wrong. Candidate factors are correlated, sometimes above 0.9. Under dependence, Bonferroni over-corrects and BH remains valid but conservative. A bootstrap of the maximum statistic would be the defensible upgrade.
  • No economics. Trading costs, capacity, borrow, taxes and crowding are all absent. Statistical survival is necessary, not sufficient — a factor can clear t > 3 and still lose money after costs.
  • The decay is imposed, not discovered. The post-publication window is harsher because the generator was told to make it harsher. It demonstrates the mechanism and the consequence; it does not prove the effect.
