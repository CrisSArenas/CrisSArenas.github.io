Agentic AI · institutional investing

Thirteen AI agents watching a $1bn portfolio, and a human who still signs off.

A large endowment or pension fund must keep its portfolio inside a rulebook: how much in each asset class, how much risk, how much it can sell in a hurry, how much in any one company. Checking that every day is slow, repetitive and unforgiving work, a good fit for agents. This page runs a fleet of specialised agents over a synthetic portfolio, shows what they find when markets move, and lets you decide what they are allowed to do about it. Most of the engineering is about controls: what the agents may do alone, what stops them, and how every action can be proven afterwards.

Step 4 of 6

What the agents found

Every finding names the rule it broke, the numbers that broke it, and the agent that raised it, so a wrong call can be traced to one agent and fixed without touching the rest of the fleet. Checks are plain arithmetic in code, never a language model guessing: rules like these must be testable and cannot be allowed to hallucinate.

Look-through: single companies

Stocks and bonds in the same company count together. That is why a portfolio can sit neatly inside every asset-class range and still hold too much of one name.

Step 5 of 6

You decide

For each finding the planner drafted a fix, costed it, and the compliance agent re-ran every rule on the portfolio as it would look after the trade, so a fix that breaks something else never reaches you. Two gates cannot be overridden: stale prices stop all automatic trading, and private assets cannot be sold to fix a range, so those go to the investment committee instead.

Step 6 of 6

The audit trail, and the bill

An agent that acted is not enough for a regulated investor; it has to be an agent that can be shown to have acted. Every finding, decision and trade is written to a hash-chained log: each line’s fingerprint is computed from the line and the fingerprint before it, so changing any entry breaks every fingerprint after it. Guide: Agentic Portfolio Monitoring

1 · What the page does

Portfolio monitoring suits agents and not a chatbot: the work is continuous, rule-bound, spread across many data sources, and the output is a short list of things a person must decide. It is also where a mistake is expensive and an unexplained action is unacceptable, so most of the engineering here is about controls, not about the model.

The walkthrough: look at the portfolio and its rulebook, hit it with real-world events, run the fleet, read the findings, approve or reject the fixes, and finally check that the record of everything that happened cannot be quietly changed.

3 · Autonomy, and the gates above it

Observe only detects and explains and never releases anything, how a deployment starts, while you measure how often the agents are right. Propose, you approve drafts, costs and re-checks every fix and waits for a click. Small trades run automatically lets the router execute fixes under $25m that are not critical and pass the post-trade check, then re-checks the portfolio after each one.

Two gates cannot be overridden from the page. Stale prices stop all automatic trading at every setting: an agent acting confidently on yesterday’s price is the most expensive failure in this design and the cheapest to prevent. Private assets cannot be sold to fix a range, so those findings go to the investment committee instead of becoming an order nobody could fill.

4 · The loop closes

Every approval changes the portfolio, so the fleet checks it again straight away. Fixes whose finding has already been cleared are withdrawn, selling emerging-market stocks into cash, for example, also lifts cash above its floor, so the separate cash top-up is no longer needed. Fixes whose finding is still open are re-sized to the new portfolio.

6 · What is simulated

No model is called and no network request leaves the page. The agents are deterministic functions with fixed latencies and token counts; each finding’s wording is generated from the rule that fired and the numbers that fired it. What is real is the architecture: the dependency graph, the gates, the post-trade check, the re-check loop, the routing and the hash chain. The portfolio is synthetic, and model prices are round illustrative numbers.

Methodology & assumptions

What this models

A production-shaped control loop for agentic portfolio oversight. Thirteen role-scoped agents run as a dependency graph over a synthetic $1.0bn multi-asset portfolio: six independent checkers cover policy ranges, tracking error, liquidity, single-company concentration, eligibility and data quality; triage ranks the findings; a planner drafts fixes; an estimator costs them; a compliance simulator re-runs every rule on the post-trade portfolio; a router applies the autonomy policy and its gates; and a logger writes a hash-chained record. Events change the portfolio for real, approvals change it again, and every change triggers a fresh check.

Key assumptions

  • Eight asset classes with targets 25/15/10/25/5/10/8/2 and per-class ranges; private equity and real assets are illiquid and capped at 25% together.
  • Company exposure is looked through: stock held in US equity plus bonds held in core bonds, each moving with its sleeve.
  • Liquidity is the share of NAV sellable inside 10 business days, against a 70% floor. “Days to sell 90%” could never pass with 14% in privates, and a limit that never passes is noise, not a control.
  • Tracking error is √(a′Σa) on active weights against the target mix, with a fixed long-run covariance.
  • Trading cost is a half-spread plus a square-root impact term per asset class: about 4 bp on US equity up to about 38 bp on high yield. Privates are not tradeable at all.
  • Agent latencies and token counts are fixed per role. A cycle takes as long as the longest chain of agents; its cost is the sum of every agent’s tokens at its tier’s price.
  • The audit fingerprint is 32-bit FNV-1a over the previous fingerprint plus the entry. It demonstrates chaining; anything real would use SHA-256 and sign the head of the chain.

Design decisions worth naming

  • Checks are code; only planning is generative. Limit checks are arithmetic, where they are testable and cannot hallucinate. The model drafts fixes, and its drafts are re-checked by the same deterministic rules.
  • The post-trade check is not optional. Fixing one breach often opens another; funding a top-up out of cash breaks the cash floor. The simulator catches it before a person ever sees the proposal.
  • Stale data gates action, not detection. Old prices should not stop you looking, only acting.
  • The loop re-plans after every change. Proposals made redundant by an earlier approval are withdrawn; the rest are re-sized.
  • Every finding names its agent. That makes the false-positive rate measurable per role, so one agent can be tuned or retired without touching the rest.

Limitations: read these before you read the numbers

  • No model is called anywhere on this page. It demonstrates an architecture and its controls, not model quality.
  • Token, latency and cost figures are illustrative. Real ones depend on prompts, context length, caching and the price list.
  • Risk is a static covariance. Correlations do not rise in a sell-off here, so tracking error after a shock is understated.
  • Trading costs are a stylised function, not a broker quote, with no settlement, tax lots, lot sizes or wash-sale rules.
  • The false-positive rate is not modelled. In a real deployment it decides whether the fleet is used or ignored, and you only learn it by running in observe-only mode against a human baseline for months.
